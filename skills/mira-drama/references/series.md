# Series, episode, hook, payoff

The story half of a short drama. Everything here is about what the viewer feels and when; the
next files turn it into shots.

## Format first

Decide three numbers before any plot: episodes, seconds per episode, clips per episode. Typical
Mira formats:

| Format | Episodes | Per episode | Clips |
|---|---|---|---|
| single scene | 1 | 15-30 s | 1-3 |
| Reels mini-series | 3-5 | 30-60 s | 3-8 |
| full short series | 6-10 | 45-90 s | 5-12 |

Long app serials of 60-100 episodes with paid walls do not fit a social feed. Where those
formats place a paid wall, a feed series places the strongest hook of the season and a "part 2"
prompt to follow.

## The season curve

Four phases, measured in episodes, not minutes:

| Phase | Share | In a 5-episode series | Job |
|---|---|---|---|
| set-up | ~15% | episode 1 | the wound, the want, the first humiliation or threat |
| rise | ~30% | episodes 2-3 | two steps forward, one back; the first small payoff |
| storm | ~35% | episodes 3-4 | the reversal, the lowest point, the hidden enemy surfaces |
| final | ~20% | episode 5 | the decisive confrontation, the biggest payoff, an open thread |

Tempo per phase, push to breath: about 6:4 in the set-up, 7:3 in the rise, 8:2 in the storm,
9:1 in the final clash, then 3:7 in the last aftermath. The lead's low point lasts at most two
episodes; three episodes without the plot moving loses the audience. Treat these as starting
points, not law.

## The first five seconds of every episode

- second 1: an image that stops the scroll, a striking action or a question (a slap, a stranger
  at the door, a ring thrown in a glass);
- by second 3: the core conflict or the status contrast is visible;
- by second 5: the viewer has a question they want answered.

Episode 2 onward first answers the previous hook within about five seconds, then poses a new
question. Open every episode as if it were someone's first.

Never open on a voice-over about the backstory, a slow landscape, the lead's ordinary morning,
"once upon a time", or more than three characters at once.

Openings that work (pick by genre):
- public humiliation: the lead is put down in front of witnesses;
- status reversal shown, not told: the doorman is the owner;
- edge of ruin: a second before the fall, then the rescue or the second chance;
- end first: the climax for two seconds, then "three days earlier";
- an action already in progress and dangerous;
- the viewer sees the decisive detail before the characters do.

For thrillers and quiet dramas three softer entries also hold: an unsettling place, one short
voice line over a contrasting image, an everyday hitch that turns wrong.

## Episode skeleton

Clear starting situation (it inherits the last episode's end state) → a crisis pushes the lead
toward a goal → the move meets a counter-move and a price → a visible result for this episode →
a new decision, threat or question → a hard end point.

The "therefore / but" test: adjacent beats must read as "because A, therefore B, but C, therefore
D". If two beats can only be joined by "and then", a choice or a consequence is missing.

## The end-of-episode hook

A hook asks a question the viewer needs answered and withholds the answer. The decision to watch
the next episode is made in the last ten seconds.

Five types:
1. **Mystery:** who is it, what did they find, which will she choose, who just walked in.
2. **Reversal:** the ally was the enemy, the victim was the plotter. At most 5-7 per season.
3. **Emotional cliff:** the confession interrupted, the goodbye at the door, the tear held back.
4. **Half-truth:** part of a secret is revealed and the part that matters is not.
5. **Sudden threat:** danger enters the last frame.

Pairs that work: mystery + emotion, reversal + threat, half-truth + mystery, threat + emotion.
Pairs that do not: two reversals in a row, two half-truths in a row, three types at once. Do not
use the same type twice within three episodes.

End on the peak. Cut on the beat that both closes this episode's conflict and opens the new
trouble; if the emotional peak and the new crisis are separate events, bring them into the same
second. Pay out this episode's result first, then hang the hook.

Bad hooks: an episode with none; a mystery solved in the first seconds of the next episode (the
viewer feels cheated); a hook unrelated to the main plot; a hook whose answer is obvious; a line
cut mid-word ("I-") with nothing visual. Models render a sentence cut short poorly; end on a
visible action instead: the hand reaching, the door opening, the screen lighting up.

## Payoff beats

Payoff is pressure released. Without pressure built first it feels cheap.

Five types:
1. **Status reveal:** the underestimated one is shown for who they are.
2. **Revenge:** the one who did harm gets it back.
3. **Comeback:** out of the pit, the lead wins.
4. **Emotional breakthrough:** the truth said, the embrace, the forgiveness.
5. **Secret unveiled:** the hidden fact comes out.

What makes a payoff land:
- witnesses and their reactions (shoot the reactions; they are half the payoff);
- the lead stays calm (the calmer, the sweeter);
- the reveal staged as a small ritual (the envelope, the phone turned around, the name said);
- the antagonist's cost shown on screen;
- a breath of aftermath before the next hook.

Rotation: never the same type twice in a row; alternate power payoffs with emotional ones; a
small payoff before a big one; payoffs grow over the season and the largest comes last.

## Antagonists

A ladder, smallest to largest: the everyday obstacle (a rude colleague), the mid-level enemy (a
rival, an ex), the true opponent (the one behind it), and optionally the hidden enemy (the
friend).

Every antagonist answers three questions: why do they do it, where does their power come from,
why have they not struck before. A mid-level enemy falls in three rounds: a draw, the lead loses,
the turn. A hidden enemy is prepared with at least three clues, one per phase, each fair on
rewatch. Make them hateful through action: hurting the weak, repaying good with harm, humiliating
in public.

## Dialogue as action

- A line is a move, not information: the same "the door closes at nine" can be a hurry, a threat
  or a defence. Write which.
- Subtext in three layers: what is said, what is wanted, why it cannot be said straight.
- Never repeat in a line what the picture just showed.
- Break a long speech with a visible action at the point where the tactic changes; that action is
  also where the shot cuts.
- Give the heaviest line to the listener's reaction, not to the speaker's face.
- Enter a scene late, leave early. After a big decision leave about two seconds for the reaction.
- English lines: the conclusion first, the conditions after; "you", not titles.

## Adapting a familiar formula

Formats from other markets carry local clichés. Keep the skeleton (underestimated → revealed;
pressure → release) and replace the surface: family duty becomes money or career pressure,
kneeling becomes a public apology, a slap becomes a verbal takedown or a resource the rival does
not have. Shorten the endurance phase for Western feeds.

## Platform lines

Even fictional, these fail review on Meta and TikTok or turn the audience: violence or forced
intimacy framed as romance, stalking framed as care, a step-by-step method for a real crime,
close-up gore. Imply, cut away, show the consequence.
