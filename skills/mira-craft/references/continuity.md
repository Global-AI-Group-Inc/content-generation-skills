# Continuity, count and cause

CONTINUITY, COUNT AND CAUSE - fold these into the prompt whenever the clip has props, more than one person, or an object that changes hands.
(1) COUNT EVERY ENTITY. Before writing the beats, fix how many of each thing exists, and write those counts into the prompt as plain statements: exactly one protagonist, exactly one passer-by, exactly one cup, exactly one phone. A thing that is never counted is a thing the model is free to duplicate, and duplication is the single most common failure in a multi-prop shot. Count people, animals, hero props and anything held, worn or carried. Only crowds and set dressing stay uncounted - name those as a mass ('background customers'), not as individuals.
(2) EVERY OBJECT HAS A CARRIER, AND A STATE AFTER IT LEAVES. Say who or what holds each object at the start - which hand, which pocket, worn where. When an object leaves its carrier, say so explicitly AND state what the carrier is left with: an emptied hand is empty and stays empty, anatomically normal, with nothing replacing what it held and no new object appearing in it later in the clip. Without that closing statement the model refills the hand on the next beat.
(3) WHAT IS HIDDEN ALREADY EXISTS. An object or feature that is covered - a face behind a mask, eyes behind lenses, a label turned away, a hand inside a pocket - is not created at the moment it is revealed; it is already there, complete and unchanged, underneath. Say that in the prompt when a reveal happens: the face already exists under the mask and does not regenerate when it comes off, the eyes behind the glasses are the same eyes before and after. Otherwise the model invents a new face at the reveal and the character changes identity mid-clip.
(4) EVERY VISIBLE CHANGE NEEDS A NAMED CAUSE. This extends the camera's motivation rule to everything in frame. An object moves because something pushed, released, lifted or dropped it; a liquid escapes because a real opening exists; a person recoils because they were touched; something grows larger in frame because the CAMERA came closer, never because it silently rescaled. Name the cause in the same sentence as the effect. Changes without a stated cause read as glitches, and the model renders them as glitches.
(5) GRAVITY OWNS WATER, SAND AND DEBRIS. Whatever a body throws up obeys gravity and inertia, not the body: water pours off a body that leaves the sea and falls back in heavy sheets, spray is flung up and drops behind a moving body instead of travelling with it, sand and dust thrown by an impact hang for a beat and settle. A column or jet of water rises only where something pumps it. Write this physics into the beat whenever a body breaks, leaves or strikes water, sand, snow or dust - the model otherwise glues the splash to the body like a rocket plume.

## When the model cannot honour everything

FIDELITY PRIORITY - the order in which things are protected when the model cannot honour everything at once. This is NOT the length-trimming order (that one lives in the output rules); it is what the PROMPT must protect first when its demands compete. In descending order: (1) the identity of the locked character - face structure, hair, build, age, marks; (2) anatomy that reads as broken when wrong - eyes, hands, the number of limbs and fingers; (3) the hero product's silhouette, colours and lettering; (4) object count and continuity - one of each thing, nothing spawned, nothing vanished; (5) the stated action and its timing; (6) camera move and framing; (7) lighting scheme and grade; (8) atmosphere, secondary motion and material adjectives. When two requirements collide, write the prompt so the higher one wins, and drop the lower one rather than hedging both - a hedged prompt loses both.

## Storyboard method

STORYBOARD METHOD - work through these six points and fold ALL of them into the final prompt. (1) OPENING FRAME: what the viewer sees in the very first frame - subject, product, wardrobe, setting, shot size (macro, close-up, medium, full body, wide) and camera angle. (2) BEATS AND TIMELINE: the ordered events that fill the clip, each pinned to an explicit stretch of seconds. Cover the clip from second zero to its final second with no gap and no overrun, roughly one beat per 2-3 seconds. Every beat names its time span, who or what moves, which body part or element moves, and how far or how fast - 'the lid tips open a few centimetres', 'she shifts her weight onto her back foot'. The MODEL section below fixes HOW that timing is written - bracketed timestamps, named seconds, labelled shots with durations, or plain in-sentence phrasing - but a timeline must be there in every prompt, never left implicit. Keep the beats inside ONE unbroken take unless the MODEL section explicitly allows separate shots. (3) CAMERA: one deliberate move per shot, named with real camera vocabulary and a pacing word - slow push-in, smooth dolly left, steady tracking shot, gentle handheld follow, crane up, orbit around, or a locked-off static camera on a tripod. Say where the move starts and where it ends. (4) SECONDARY MOTION: what else is alive in the frame - hair, fabric, steam, liquid, dust, foliage, reflections, a highlight travelling across a surface. This is what separates a video from a still. (5) LOOK: lighting setup and direction, color palette, lens character, depth of field, overall mood. (6) CLOSING FRAME: where the subject and the camera come to rest at the end. Quantify motion ('slowly', 'halfway', 'a few centimetres', 'at natural speed') instead of abstract mood words. Every clip must carry at least ONE clear, gentle body movement - a soft turn toward camera, a weight shift, a relaxed sway, a slow arm gesture, a nod, a smile forming - a frozen statue is a failed clip. Keep that movement slow, smooth and physically plausible, and push the extra energy into the camera and the secondary motion. Steer away from running, jumping, fast spins, sports action and fine-motor detail (spraying, sipping, eating, typing, finger gestures), and keep readable text (labels, packaging copy, screens) flat, stable and legible - current video models mangle all of these. Where the MODEL section forbids negation, phrase such constraints positively by naming what stays calm and still. PROP CONTINUITY: every prop, garment and accessory exists in EXACTLY ONE instance for the whole clip. When an object moves - a cigar going from the hand to the mouth, a glass lifted from the table, a phone taken out of a pocket - it LEAVES the place it was in: describe the move as one continuous path for that single object. Never let one object be in two places in the same frame (held in the hand AND still standing on the table), never spawn a fresh copy when a new beat starts, and never let a prop vanish and reappear. The same rule holds for people: only the characters named in the opening frame are in the clip, and no extra person walks into frame later.
